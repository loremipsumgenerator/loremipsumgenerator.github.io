Lorem Ipsum Copyright and Licensing

Is lorem ipsum free to use in commercial work? What about themed generators and modern filler text? The legal basics.

The classic text is public domain

The classic lorem ipsum passage is in the public domain. It is derived from a text written in 45 BC, scrambled by printers in the 1500s, and popularized in the 1960s. Nobody owns it, nobody can claim copyright over it, and you can use it in any project, commercial or otherwise, without permission, payment or attribution. That freedom is one reason it has survived for so long.

Themed generators and their terms

Themed generators are a different matter. The words they use may be original, and the generator's website may have terms of use that restrict commercial use or require attribution. In practice, short filler text is unlikely to be the subject of a legal dispute, but if you use a themed generator in a commercial product, check its terms first. When in doubt, use the classic passage, which has no terms at all.

Modern filler and AI-generated text

Some modern filler is written by a person or generated by software, and it may be protected by copyright if it is original enough. A paragraph of generic filler is unlikely to meet the threshold of originality, but a distinctive, creative passage might. If you use filler text from a specific source in a commercial product, check the source's licence. For most design work, the classic text or your own neutral filler avoids the question entirely.

Using filler in products you sell

If you sell templates, themes or mockups that contain placeholder text, the classic passage is the safest choice, because buyers can use it freely. If you use themed or modern filler, make sure your licence covers it, or replace it with the classic text before you sell. Buyers should never have to worry that the sample text in a template they bought could cause a legal problem.

When to ask a lawyer

This guide is general information, not legal advice. For most design work, the classic lorem ipsum passage raises no legal issues at all. If you are building a product that depends on a specific body of text, or if you receive a complaint about text you used, consult a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction. For everyday mockups and templates, the public-domain classic is the safe, simple choice.

Attribution and licences in templates

If you sell templates, include a short note in the documentation that explains which sample text you used and that it is free to use or must be replaced. Buyers appreciate clarity, and it protects you if a buyer asks where the text came from. For the classic passage, a single sentence is enough: 'Sample text is lorem ipsum, which is in the public domain.' For any other filler, state the source and its licence, so the buyer can make an informed decision.
