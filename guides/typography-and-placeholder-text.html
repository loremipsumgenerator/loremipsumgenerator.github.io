Typography and Placeholder Text

How placeholder text helps you test fonts, line length, leading and hierarchy — and where it misleads you.

Testing typefaces with filler

Lorem ipsum is the traditional text for testing a typeface, because it shows the full range of lowercase letters without distracting the reader with meaning. Set the same passage in two candidate fonts at the same size and compare them: which is easier to read, which has better spacing, which feels right for the brand? Look at the texture of the paragraph, not just the shape of individual letters, because texture is what readers actually experience.

Line length and measure

Placeholder text lets you test line length, the number of characters per line, which is one of the most important typographic choices. The comfortable range for body text is roughly 45 to 75 characters per line. Generate a paragraph, set it in your chosen size, and count the characters on a line. If the line is too long, readers lose their place; if it is too short, the eye jumps too often. Adjust the column width or the font size until the measure feels right.

Leading and spacing

Leading, the space between lines, is hard to judge with a single line of text. A paragraph of placeholder text shows whether the lines are too tight, which feels cramped, or too loose, which feels disconnected. Test at least three paragraphs together, because the spacing between paragraphs matters as much as the spacing within them. A good rule is leading of about 1.5 times the font size for body text, adjusted to taste.

Hierarchy with filler

Placeholder text tests hierarchy: the relationship between headings, subheadings and body text. Generate a heading, a subheading and two paragraphs, and see whether the eye moves through them in the right order. If the heading does not stand out, it needs more size, weight or space. If the subheading competes with the heading, it needs less. Hierarchy is judged by the eye, and filler text gives the eye something realistic to judge.

Where filler misleads typography

Placeholder text can mislead typography in one way: its even texture hides problems with specific characters. Real copy contains capitals, numbers, punctuation, long words and short words, all of which affect spacing and wrapping. Once you have chosen a typeface with filler, test it again with real copy, including the longest word and the most awkward punctuation your content will contain. A typeface that survives real copy is the right choice.

Choosing type for long reading

For long reading, such as articles and books, the choice of typeface matters more than for short labels. A serif face with generous spacing often reads more comfortably in long passages, while a clean sans-serif suits short interface text. Test both with several paragraphs of placeholder text at the size your readers will use, and read them yourself at a normal distance. The typeface that feels effortless after three paragraphs is the right one for long reading, even if another looks better in a headline.

Frequently asked questions

How many characters per line is ideal?

About 45 to 75 characters for body text. Shorter lines suit narrow columns; longer lines suit wide screens with large type.

What leading should I use?

Around 1.5 times the font size is a good starting point for body text. Adjust it until the paragraph feels comfortable.

Can placeholder text choose a font?

It can narrow the choice, but always confirm with real copy, which contains the capitals, numbers and punctuation your content needs.

Serif or sans-serif for long articles?

Serif faces often read more comfortably in long passages, but the best test is to read three paragraphs yourself at normal distance.
